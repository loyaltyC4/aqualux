What Makes a Light Good for Planted Tanks?

You need to understand three things: PAR, spectrum, and intensity. Ignore vague "wattage per gallon" rules. They are useless.

PAR: Photosynthetically Active Radiation

PAR measures the amount of light plants actually use for photosynthesis. It's not about how bright the light looks to your eyes. It's about the light energy hitting your plants. A good light delivers enough PAR to the substrate, where most plants root.

  • Low Light: 15-30 PAR at the substrate. Good for Anubias, Java Fern, Mosses.
  • Medium Light: 30-60 PAR at the substrate. Suits most stem plants, Cryptocorynes, Swords.
  • High Light: 60+ PAR at the substrate. For demanding plants, red plants, carpeting plants. Requires CO2 injection.

Without a PAR meter (they are expensive), you rely on manufacturer claims or experienced hobbyist recommendations. Be skeptical. More expensive does not always mean higher PAR where it matters.

Spectrum: The Colors Plants Use

Plants primarily use red and blue light for photosynthesis. Green light is mostly reflected, which is why plants appear green to us. "Full spectrum" lighting means the light emits across the entire visible spectrum, often with peaks in the red and blue ranges. This makes plants look vibrant and supports healthy growth.

  • Red Light (600-700 nm): Crucial for flowering, fruiting, and overall plant growth.
  • Blue Light (400-500 nm): Essential for vegetative growth and compact plant structure.
  • Green Light (500-600 nm): Less critical for photosynthesis but contributes to plant health and makes the tank look good.

Many cheap lights just have white LEDs. Better lights add specific red and blue diodes to enhance growth and color. The Aurora 48 Planted Tank LED Bar, for example, uses 30 white and 10 blue LEDs to provide a good balance.

Intensity and Duration

Intensity is simply how strong the light is. Duration is how long it stays on. Too much intensity or too long a duration, and you get algae. Too little, and plants melt.

Start with 6-8 hours a day. Adjust as needed. If algae appears, reduce duration or intensity. If plants are leggy or pale, increase intensity or duration slightly. This is an art, not a science.

LED vs. Other Lighting Types for Planted Tanks

LEDs are the only sensible choice for planted tanks today. Incandescent bulbs are a joke. Fluorescent tubes (T5, T8) are outdated, less efficient, and cost more to run and replace.

  • Efficiency: LEDs use less power for the same light output. This saves you money on electricity.
  • Lifespan: LEDs last for years, often a decade or more, before dimming significantly. Fluorescents need replacing annually.
  • Customization: Many LEDs offer adjustable intensity, spectrum control (RGB), and built-in timers.
  • Heat: LEDs produce less heat than other options, which helps keep your tank temperature stable.
  • Size: LEDs are compact and sleek, fitting better with modern aquarium aesthetics.

Don't bother with anything but LEDs. They are better in every way.

Understanding Light Features: Are They Worth the Money?

Modern LED lights come with a range of features. Some are genuinely useful; others are just fluff.

Built-in Timers

Essential. Plants need a consistent light cycle. A built-in timer, like on the Aurora 48 Planted Tank LED Bar, saves you from buying an external one. If your light doesn't have one, get a cheap mechanical timer from a hardware store. It's non-negotiable.

RGB Color Control and App Control

Lights like the Aurora Pro RGB, Bluetooth, IP68 and Aurora Smart 78, RGB App-Controlled LED offer RGB control. This means you can adjust the red, green, and blue channels independently. It's great for:

  • Fine-tuning spectrum: Optimize for specific plant types or growth phases.
  • Aesthetics: Boost reds for vibrant plant colors or shift to warmer/cooler tones.
  • Sunrise/Sunset modes: Gradually brighten and dim, reducing stress for fish and mimicking natural cycles.

Is it necessary? No. Is it nice to have for a high-end tank? Absolutely. For a basic tank, a simple white/blue light is fine. Don't pay extra for RGB if you just want things to grow.

Dimming and Intensity Control

Very useful. Being able to dim your light lets you adjust for algae outbreaks or new plant setups. It's a key tool for light management. Most app-controlled lights include this. Cheaper lights might have simple high/low settings. Any control is better than none.

Waterproofing (IP Rating)

An IP rating indicates how resistant a device is to dust and water. For aquarium lights:

  • IP44: Splash resistant. Fine for lights sitting on the tank rim, but don't submerge them. The Aurora Smart 78 is IP44.
  • IP68: Fully submersible. This is the gold standard for safety around water. The Aurora Pro RGB has an IP68 rating. It means the light is silicone sealed and can withstand being fully submerged. This is a good feature for peace of mind, especially if you have an open-top tank or are prone to splashing during maintenance.

You don't need a submersible light if it sits securely on the rim and you're careful. But IP68 offers an extra layer of protection.

Avoiding Common Lighting Mistakes

Getting the right light is only half the battle. Using it correctly is critical.

Too Much Light

The most common mistake. Too much light without enough CO2 and nutrients leads to rampant algae. You are trying to grow plants, not green slime. If your plants are melting and algae is exploding, your light is probably too strong or on for too long. Reduce intensity or duration. For new setups, start low and slow.

Inconsistent Photoperiod

Plants thrive on consistency. An erratic light schedule stresses them and can lead to algae. Use a timer. Every day, the light should come on and go off at the same time.

Ignoring Maintenance

Algae will grow on your light bar and diffuser. Wipe it clean regularly. A dirty light means less light reaching your plants. It's simple maintenance, but often overlooked.

Not Understanding Your Plants' Needs

Don't try to grow high-light plants in a low-light tank. Research your plants. Match your light to what you want to grow. Trying to force a carpet of Glossostigma with a basic desk lamp is a waste of time and money.
