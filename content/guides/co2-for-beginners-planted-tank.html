How do I set up a basic pressurised CO2 system?

Follow these steps. All parts are from the list above, so you won't need extra items.

  1. Mount the refillable CO2 cylinder on a sturdy base near the tank.
  2. Attach the Dual‑Gauge Regulator + Solenoid to the cylinder valve. Tighten by hand, then give a quarter turn with a wrench.
  3. Connect Airline Tubing to the regulator's outlet. Push the tubing onto the barbed fitting until it seats fully.
  4. Secure the Inline Atomiser Diffuser to the other end of the tubing. The diffuser fits 16/22 mm tubing; the provided tubing is compatible.
  5. Place the diffuser near the substrate, preferably at the back of the tank where water flow is gentle.
  6. Install the Glass Drop Checker on the tank glass using the suction cup. Fill the chamber with the supplied 4 dKH solution.
  7. Open the cylinder valve slowly. Adjust the working pressure gauge until you see a steady stream of bubbles from the diffuser. Typical beginner settings are 1.0–1.2 psi.
  8. Turn on the solenoid (or connect a timer) so CO2 runs only when the lights are on.

After the first day, watch the drop checker. If the solution turns blue, CO2 is too low. If it turns green, you are near saturation. Aim for a light blue color, indicating about 20–30 ppm CO2.

How do I monitor and adjust CO2 levels?

The drop checker is the simplest visual tool. It reacts within minutes, letting you fine‑tune the regulator on the spot. For more precise control, add a Bubble Counter + Check Valve Set. The glass counter shows bubbles per minute; most beginners target 1–2 bubbles per second (60–120 bpm) for a 20‑gal tank.

Adjust the regulator by turning the small knob on the working‑pressure gauge. Small turns change the flow noticeably. Remember:

  • Increase flow if the drop checker stays clear or pale green.
  • Decrease flow if the solution turns deep green or if fish show signs of stress.
  • After any water change, re‑check the gauge. New water can shift pH and affect CO2 solubility.

Use the solenoid valve to shut off CO2 when lights are off. This prevents excess buildup and saves gas.

Is a DIY CO2 generator a good alternative for beginners?

The DIY kit looks attractive because the upfront price is $65.95, lower than a regulator‑diffuser combo. However, the chemical reaction that produces CO2 is less stable than a pressurised cylinder. The kit delivers up to 3 kg of CO2, far more than a 20‑gal tank needs. Over‑production can cause pH crashes and fish loss.

If you already have a reliable cylinder, the DIY system adds unnecessary risk. If you cannot afford a cylinder, start with the regulator‑diffuser combo and buy a small 2‑lb cylinder later. The cylinder cost is a one‑time expense; refills are cheap and predictable.

In short, the DIY option is only worth it for hobbyists who enjoy tinkering and can monitor pH constantly. For most beginners, the starter kit or a regulator‑diffuser pair is the safer, more consistent choice.
